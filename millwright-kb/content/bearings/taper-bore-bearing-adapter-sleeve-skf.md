> **Why this matters.** A tapered-bore spherical roller bearing (suffix **K**) does not get its fit from the shaft tolerance. *You* create the interference fit by driving the inner ring up the taper. Drive too little and the ring creeps and frets on the sleeve. Drive too far and you use up the internal clearance, the rollers bind, and the bearing runs hot and fails early. The **SKF reduction card** tells you how much clearance to remove for each bore size.

## What you need

- Feeler gauge set (long blades, 0.001" or 0.02 mm steps, or metric 0.03-0.05 steps)
- Hook spanner (or SKF/impact spanner) that fits the KM lock nut, or a hydraulic nut (HMV) for large bores
- Light machine oil, clean rags, solvent
- Calipers or a micrometer to record shaft diameter
- Dial indicator on a magnetic base (for axial drive-up method)
- The bearing's designation and the housing (pillow block / SAF / SNL) instructions

## Step 0 - Identify the bearing and taper

Read the suffix on the bearing:

![Double-row spherical roller bearing: barrel rollers on one spherical outer raceway](/photos/bearings/spherical-roller-cutaway.jpg)

*Double-row spherical roller bearing: barrel rollers on one spherical outer raceway. Photo: Silberwolf, CC BY 2.5, via commons*

| Suffix | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **K** | Tapered bore, taper 1:12 (most 222xx / 223xx / 230xx / 231xx / 232xx series) |
| **K30** | Tapered bore, taper 1:30 (240xx / 241xx series) |
| **C3** | Radial internal clearance greater than Normal (very common on K bearings) |
| **C4** | Clearance greater than C3 |
| **E / CC / CCK / EK** | SKF internal-design codes; do not change the procedure |

Bore diameter is the last two digits × 5 for codes 04 and up. **22220 EK C3** → bore 20 × 5 = **100 mm**, taper 1:12, C3 clearance. The matching adapter sleeve is **H 320** (for a 100 mm bore bearing on a 90 mm shaft; the sleeve number is the bearing's last two digits with the sleeve series in front).

## Step 1 - Measure and record the unmounted radial internal clearance

![Measuring clearance over the top roller on an adapter sleeve mounting](/img/bearings/adapter-sleeve-drive-up.svg)

*Measuring clearance over the top roller on an adapter sleeve mounting*

1. Wipe preservative off the bearing but do **not** wash it. Leave the factory oil film.
2. Stand the bearing upright on a clean bench. Spin the inner ring a couple of turns so the rollers seat against the guide ribs.
3. Push the inner ring down (or let gravity load it) so all the clearance sits at the **top** (12 o'clock).
4. Slide feeler blades between the outer-ring raceway and the **unloaded roller at the top** of **both** rows. Push the blade over the full length of the roller. The biggest blade that passes with light drag is the reading. If the two rows differ, use the smaller value.
5. **Write it down.** This is your starting clearance. It should fall inside the unmounted C3 (or Normal) band for that bore. If it does not, stop and find out why (wrong bearing, damage, mixed-up parts).

> Rotate the outer ring a few degrees and measure again. Take the average if the readings differ a little. The important number is the *change* you are about to make.

## Step 2 - Prepare the shaft and sleeve

1. Measure the shaft where the sleeve will sit. Adapter-sleeve shafts are usually **h9** tolerance with a cylindricity of IT5/2. A cheap commercial shaft that is out of round or worn will let the sleeve walk.
2. Check the shaft for burrs, raised keyway edges and rust. Stone lightly. Solvent-clean and wipe dry.
3. Clean the sleeve, lock nut and lock washer. Light oil on the sleeve's outside taper and the nut face and threads. **No oil on the bore of the sleeve or the shaft** if the application must resist axial thrust; a dry sleeve-to-shaft interface grips better.
4. Slide the sleeve onto the shaft **threaded end pointing outward**, away from the bearing's final position, so the nut can draw the bearing up the taper. Position it roughly where the bearing centre must end up. Remember the bearing moves axially along the shaft as it is driven up; allow for that.

## Step 3 - Set the bearing to "line-to-line"

1. Slide the bearing onto the sleeve, large end of the bore toward the nut.
2. Put on the lock washer with its inner tab in the sleeve keyway, then the lock nut with its chamfered side toward the washer.
3. Tighten the nut by hand, then with the spanner just until the bearing seats fully on the taper and all play is gone. This is **zero** or **line-to-line**. Mark the nut and the sleeve with a marker so you can count the turning angle later.

## Step 4 - Drive up and reduce the clearance

Choose **one** of the SKF methods. In school and in the field the first one is the standard.

### Method 1 - Clearance reduction (feeler gauge)

1. Tighten the nut in small steps with the hook spanner (or hydraulic nut).
2. After each step re-measure the top-roller clearance at both rows exactly as in Step 1.
3. Stop when the clearance has dropped by the amount in the card below (**Reduction of radial internal clearance**). Aim for the middle of the range.
4. Check that what is left is **not less than the minimum residual clearance** for the bearing's clearance class (see the FAG inch table further down for the "after mounting" minimums). If it is, you have gone too far. Back the nut off, drive again to the correct value; if the bearing has already been over-driven several times it should be replaced.

### Method 2 - Lock nut tightening angle

Only listed for bores up to 100 mm. From the line-to-line mark, turn the nut through the angle in the card (100°-150°). Then **verify with feeler gauges** anyway.

### Method 3 - Axial drive-up distance

Set a dial indicator against the bearing inner ring face and drive the bearing the distance **s** in the card. With an SKF hydraulic nut (HMV ..E) the drive-up is read from the nut's dial gauge directly. Valid only on solid steel shafts.

## Step 5 - Lock it

1. Back the nut off just enough (never more than one tab) to line a slot with a lock-washer tab. **Do not** back off more than the small angle needed; that loses drive-up.
2. Bend the tab into the slot with a punch. Bend a **different** tab than one that has been bent before; a fatigued tab breaks.
3. Wipe the assembly. Re-check that the outer ring turns freely and the rollers are not tight.

## Step 6 - Housing, seals, lubricant

- For split housings (SNL / SAF / SN), the bearing must be centred so the cap does not pinch. Locating rings go on the fixed (held) bearing only; the free bearing must be able to float.
- Fill grease to the housing instructions (usually 1/3 to 1/2 of the free space), or oil to the sight-glass mark.
- Record the final clearance, date, bearing number and sleeve number on the work order. The next millwright will thank you.

## Worked example

Bearing **22222 EK C3** on a **H 322** sleeve, 100 mm shaft (bearing bore 110 mm, band 100-120).

| Step | Reading |
|---|---|
| Unmounted clearance measured at top rollers | 0.15 mm (inside C3 band of 0.135-0.170 for this size) |
| Target reduction from SKF card (100-120 mm) | 0.050 - 0.060 mm |
| Target mounted clearance | 0.15 - 0.055 = **about 0.095 mm** |
| Minimum permitted residual (C3, 100-120) | 0.065 mm (.0026") |
| Result | 0.095 mm ≥ 0.065 mm → **pass** |

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring the bottom rollers.** Clearance shows at the *unloaded* row position. With the bearing standing on the bench that is the top.
- **Washing out the factory oil**, then measuring "dry". Readings change.
- **Driving up by feel.** A 100 mm bearing gets ruined by a strong arm on a long spanner. Measure.
- **Backing off the nut a full tab** to get the washer to line up. Re-drive instead.
- **Forgetting the bearing walks along the shaft** as it is driven up. Position the sleeve so the *final* bearing centre lands in the housing centre.
- **Using a C3 card value on a Normal-clearance bearing.** The reduction value is the same; the residual minimum is not.
